Introduction

Where History Lives on the Ganga

Buxar has never been a footnote. In the age of the epics this tract was the ashram of sages — Siddhashrama, the place of accomplished tapas — where the boy Rama broke a bow that no man could lift, and where the sage Vishwamitra led him into the woods against the demon Tadaka. The town is said to take its name from Vyaghrasrama, the hermitage of the tiger.

Standing at the great ford of the Ganga, where the old roads of Assam and Magadha crossed, Buxar was a living crossroads of armies and pilgrims. In 1539 Sher Shah Suri crushed Humayun at Chausa; in 1764 the armies of the Mughal emperor and the Nawabs of Awadh and Bengal fell to Major Munro's Company troops — a defeat that handed the Company the diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, and reshaped the subcontinent.

Battle of Chausa · 1539

The Rise of Sher Shah Suri

Ten miles south-west of Buxar, on the very bank of the Ganga, the plain of Chausa earned its place in the story of the Sur period. In 1539 the Mughal emperor Humayun marched against Sher Shah Suri after the fall of Chunar. On 26 June — the date recorded in the Chausa colophon — the rains had come, and the field was flooded.

Sher Shah's Ujjainiya Rajput allies under Gajpati Ujjainia chose the waterside, drawing the Mughals into the storm. Humayun's line broke, and the Sur troops caught the Mughals at the flooded junction of the Karamnasa and the Ganga. Some eight thousand Mughal troops perished in the waters and the rout; the camp, the artillery and the imperial ladies were taken — yet the harem was treated with honour, a mercy remembered in the Chausa colophon of the Sur scribes.

According to the Tazkirat-ul-waqiat, Humayun — who could not swim — was struggling in the torrent with his arm held out in vain. Nizam, a water-carrier, calmed the emperor: "do not struggle in the water, as you are unable to swim", and carried him across on the strength of his own arm, giving up the king to safety. For this he was raised to the rank of a Khan. The camp and the royal ladies fell to Sher Shah, who ordered that the harem be treated with honour — mercy for "those who are under the protection of God".

Battle of Buxar · 1764

The Battle That Changed India

A decisive encounter that determined the course of colonial rule in the Indian subcontinent.

1764

On the morning of 22 October 1764, the troops of Shah Alam II, Mir Qasim and Shuja-ud-Daula — the Nawab of Awadh — came out of the fort's shadow some forty thousand strong. Against them stood Major Hector Munro and fewer than 7,000 men, most of them Indian sepoys of the East India Company.

On the third day, 23 October, the decisive stroke fell. When the allied artillery scattered, Munro's disciplined cross-fire and bayonet broke the line in three. The Company listed 289 killed, 499 wounded and 85 missing; the allied armies lost over two thousand men.

A year later, at Allahabad, the diwani — the revenue administration — of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa was ceded to the Company. The old order, which would never again assemble so great an army on one field, had fallen; the story of company rule had begun.

Heritage & Spirituality

Faith on the Banks of the Ganga

On this bend of the Ganga, faith and history were written into the same landscape. The old town holds Siddhashrama, the ashram of the sage Vishwamitra where the boy Rama strung the bow; a few kilometres west, the ground at Ahirauli remembers the restoration of Ahilya. The town's old names — Siddhashrama, Vedgarbhapuri, Karush, Tapovan, Chaitratha, Vyaghrasrama — are a litany of the ways Buxar has been claimed by the ages.

Modern Buxar

A City in Renaissance

From a storied past to a growing district headquarters — a new chapter is being written.

In the century of independence the district matured. Buxar became a district of Bihar on 17 March 1991, carved from Bhojpur. The 2011 census counted over 1.7 million people in the district; the town itself is now home to well over a hundred thousand across the railway and the ghats. The National Waterway-1 glides past the old fort on the Ganga, and the mango gardens of Chausa keep a flavour that no market in the state can do without.

  • District since 17 March 1991
  • Population 1,706,352 (Census 2011)
  • National Waterway-1 on the Ganga
  • Patna–Varanasi rail corridor
  • Mango country of Chausa
Today Buxar Railway Station · photo by Vivek Rai · CC BY-SA 3.0

The Ganga still swings the fortune of the district — in grain, in mango, in the passage of the National Waterway — while the railway keeps the hours of a town that has always stood on somebody's road.
